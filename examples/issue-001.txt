THE WOMAN NOBODY ASKED TO NAME
The most reproduced photograph of the Depression, and the forty years before anyone found out who was in it

A NOTE BEFORE YOU READ
This story involves severe poverty and hunger.
She sat for ten minutes. The photographer took six frames, filed them
with a caption that recorded her age and the number of her children but
not her name, and drove on. The picture went around the world within a
week. It was another forty-two years before anyone printed who she was.

WHAT YOU ARE LOOKING AT
----------------------------------------------------------------
A woman of thirty-two in a lean-to tent at the edge of a frozen pea
field, two children turned away from the camera against her shoulders,
an infant in her lap. Her hand is at her jaw. The photograph was made in
March 1936 by Dorothea Lange, working for the Resettlement
Administration, a New Deal agency that employed photographers to
document rural poverty and, not incidentally, to argue for its own
budget. The negative is held by the Library of Congress and carries no
known restrictions on publication, which is part of why you have seen it
before.

THE STORY
----------------------------------------------------------------
Lange was driving home to Berkeley at the end of a month in the field
when she passed a hand-lettered sign for a pea-pickers' camp at Nipomo.
She drove on for twenty miles, turned around, and went back. By her own
later account she made the pictures in about ten minutes and asked
almost nothing: she did not take the woman's name, and she did not write
down what she had been told beyond the bare facts that went into the
caption.

The crop had frozen. There was no work and no pay, and the camp held
something like two thousand five hundred people with nothing to do but
wait. Lange's negatives went to Washington and then, unusually fast, to
the San Francisco News, which ran two of them within days under a story
about starvation in the camps. Federal food aid was dispatched. The
family had already moved on.

There are six exposures in the sequence, and they are worth looking at
together, because they show a photographer working out what the picture
is. The first is wide: the tent, a rocking chair, a teenage daughter,
the whole scene. Lange moves closer with each frame. Furniture
disappears. The older children turn their faces away. In the last
exposure everything that would explain the situation has been removed,
and what is left is a face.

That compression is what made the image portable. It could stand for the
Dust Bowl, for the Depression, for migrant labour, for motherhood, for
any argument that needed a face. It appeared on a postage stamp. It was
used to sell magazines and, later, to advertise things that had nothing
to do with it.

One detail was removed more literally. In the file print at the Library
of Congress, a thumb is visible at the lower right edge of the frame,
hooked around a tent pole. Before the picture went into wide circulation
the thumb was retouched out of the negative — scraped away, permanently.
The alteration was not disclosed. It is a small thing, and it is the
only physical evidence in the whole sequence that Lange, too, was
composing rather than simply recording.

The woman was Florence Owens Thompson. She was Cherokee, born in Indian
Territory in Oklahoma in 1903, and she was not a Dust Bowl migrant at
all: she had come to California in the 1920s, years before the drought.
She was identified publicly in the late 1970s, after a reporter tracked
her down. By then the photograph had been in print for four decades.

She was not pleased about it. She had received nothing for the picture
and had no say in how it was used, and she said, more than once, that
she wished Lange had never taken it.

WHY IT MATTERS
----------------------------------------------------------------
The Resettlement Administration's photography unit produced something
like a quarter of a million negatives, and the entire archive was placed
in the public domain. That decision is the reason the image was
reproduced so widely, and it is also the reason its subject had no
leverage over it. Free reuse is not a neutral property of a photograph.
It determines who gets to decide what the picture means, and in this
case the answer was: everyone except the person in it.

The caption is the other half of the story. Lange recorded an age, a
number of children and a place. A name would have cost nothing and would
have made the woman a person rather than a category. The omission was
standard practice for documentary work of the period, which is exactly
the point — it was invisible until someone went looking.

ONE MORE THING
----------------------------------------------------------------
Thompson's daughter Katherine, the child on the right, said in later
interviews that the family had been living on frozen vegetables
scavenged from the field and birds the children killed. She also said
the car in which they were sitting out the freeze had not broken down,
as the story is usually told: they had sold the tyres to buy food.
