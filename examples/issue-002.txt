THE INSTRUMENT YOU PLAY BY NOT TOUCHING IT
It began as a way to measure gas, and ended up inside the American ambassador's study in Moscow

There is no keyboard, no string, no reed and no mouthpiece. There is a
box, a vertical rod and a horizontal loop, and a performer standing in
front of it moving their hands through empty air. Nothing is touched.
The instrument was not designed to be an instrument at all.

WHAT YOU ARE LOOKING AT
----------------------------------------------------------------
A wooden cabinet on legs with two protruding antennas. The upright rod
governs pitch: the closer the player's right hand comes, the higher the
note. The loop on the left governs volume, and works in reverse — hand
near the loop means silence. Both respond to capacitance, the electrical
property of a body occupying space near a circuit. The player is one
plate of a capacitor. The instrument is the other.

THE STORY
----------------------------------------------------------------
In about 1920, at the Physico-Technical Institute in Petrograd, a young
physicist named Lev Sergeyevich Termen was building apparatus to measure
the dielectric constant of gases under varying conditions. Part of the
rig converted its readings into an audible tone, which was a common
enough trick when instruments were read by ear rather than by dial.
Termen noticed that the tone shifted as he moved his hand near the
circuit. Anyone else would have logged it as interference and shielded
the apparatus.

He worked out the range instead, added a second oscillator, and taught
himself to play it. He had trained as a cellist, which is why the
fingering he developed is closer to string technique than to keyboard
technique: there are no discrete notes to land on, only positions in the
air that have to be found by ear and held by muscle memory. He
demonstrated the instrument across the Soviet Union and then across
Europe, and by 1928 he was in New York with a US patent.

RCA licensed it and began production in 1929, marketing it as an
instrument anyone could play, which was the opposite of true. Roughly
five hundred were built. They sold badly, the Depression finished the
market, and the theremin might have ended there as a curiosity with a
short commercial life.

What saved it was a violinist. Clara Rockmore, born in Vilnius and
admitted to the St Petersburg Conservatory at five, had lost the use of
her bow arm to a tendon condition and could not continue as a violinist.
She took up the theremin in her twenties and treated it as a serious
concert instrument rather than a novelty — which meant confronting the
fact that it has no way of separating one note from the next. Her
solution was an aerial fingering technique of considerable precision,
and a series of demands on Termen for a faster volume response so that
she could articulate notes at all. He rebuilt the instrument to her
specification.

In 1938 Termen left New York and returned to the Soviet Union. The
circumstances are disputed: some accounts have him abducted by Soviet
agents from his apartment, others have him leaving of his own accord,
deep in debt and facing tax problems. What is documented is where he
ended up — in a sharashka, a prison laboratory of the kind the Soviet
state ran for scientists it wanted to use rather than waste.

WHY IT MATTERS
----------------------------------------------------------------
The theremin is usually filed as a footnote in the history of electronic
music, which undersells what it settled. It was the first instrument in
which the sound had no mechanical origin at all: no vibrating string, no
column of air, no struck surface. The note existed only as a voltage.
Every synthesiser and every sampler that followed rests on that premise,
and the theremin is where it was demonstrated in front of a paying
audience.

It also set the terms of a problem that electronic instruments have been
working on ever since. A piano gives you a note when you press a key. An
instrument controlled by a field gives you a continuum, and a continuum
is much harder to play. Almost a century of interface design — keyboards
bolted onto synthesisers, pitch wheels, ribbon controllers, touch
surfaces — is an argument about how much of that continuum a player can
actually use.

ONE MORE THING
----------------------------------------------------------------
The device Termen built in the sharashka was a listening apparatus with
no batteries, no wires and no power supply of its own: it did nothing
until a radio beam was aimed at it from outside, at which point a
membrane inside modulated the reflected signal with whatever was being
said in the room. In 1945 a group of Soviet schoolchildren presented the
US ambassador in Moscow with a carved wooden replica of the Great Seal
of the United States. He hung it in his study. It stayed there for seven
years.
